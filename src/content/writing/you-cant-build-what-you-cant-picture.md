so i was sitting in chase center this july, day two of yc startup school, a few thousand of us in an actual stadium, and sam altman said a thing i had already heard alexandr wang say the day before.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FlEs!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F33cf84aa-db56-446b-b79b-d9a6734ae939_4032x2754.jpeg)

that’s what got me. not the thing itself. the fact that it was the second time.

this is my paraphrase and not either of their words, to be clear, but the idea both of them kept circling was this. that what you actually need to build is a really strong intuition. a kind of, okay, i don’t think moral compass is exactly the right phrase for this context. an inner compass. and that compass includes everything, your morals, your ambition, your direction, all of it, and you need it built strongly enough that you’re not swayed by every new thing that appears. because you built it out of your own focused vision of what the world is right now, what’s actually possible, and how you could make something that moves us one step closer to that and means something along the way.

i sat there thinking, i know what that is. i just read a whole book about it.

## before this year i genuinely did not care about any of this

context. it was this year, grade 12, that we read northrop frye’s the educated imagination in english class.

and to be very honest, before reading that book i had a completely different view about fiction and literature. i used to really think of myself as a very ambitious person who wanted to shut out all the noise around me, all the distractions. i thought reading wasn’t really important. fictional stories especially were not really important. all of that jazz.

and then i read it and it really touched me, like, you don’t understand.

it affects the way i see the world now. it broadens your view to just how much of our world is made of repeated patterns and stories of humanity. so many truths about how we learn or don’t learn from past mistakes, how they repeat or get slightly better each time. i think that’s a very beautiful observation.

but the part that matters for what i’m actually trying to say here is the idea of exercising your imagination well enough that you can genuinely visualize a world you want to be in. not just want, *picture.* and that kind of projection requires a lot of exposure to material that helps you grasp the fundamental things about what humanity is, what we’ve done, what we’ve learned, what mindsets and ideologies we keep depending on.

which is exactly the muscle those two people on stage were describing. they just weren’t calling it that.

## the race

here’s the problem, and i say this as someone standing fully inside it.

i think this is a struggle for most people in tech. we don’t really consume that kind of media. and the reason isn’t that we’re shallow, it’s that the whole space is so fast paced, the ai landscape changes every other week, so you’re always feeling like you have to catch up. learn the tool. ship the project. stay current. which is great and all, until that becomes your one and only motivation in life, which is just to not be left behind in that tortoise hare race.

now that i have a bit of detachment from it, i think that approach is so wrong. and people carry that exact mindset into starting companies. i can’t be left behind, i need to start something.

i’m not describing other people here. one year ago i was the person who thought reading was a distraction from the real work.

## the actual argument

so if the thing you need is an inner compass for what the world is and what it’s becoming, how do you build one?

if you’re only working from the tech landscape, that just means studying what technologies existed then and what exists now. and that’s fine. but the all-encompassing version, the one those speakers were pointing at, comes from actually indulging in the history of humanity. because compared to those enormous patterns, our technological patterns are a pretty minor subset. we’re a small chapter inside a much longer thing.

and this is where i think it matters practically, not just spiritually.

someone very focused on the tech pattern looks at the current tools and thinks, okay, what will people need *next*, after this. that’s a real skill and it builds real companies. but someone who understands the broader pattern knows that our needs themselves keep moving. that the wanting changes shape. and that person has a shot at seeing what bigger thing is sitting in front of all of us that many generations of current tools won’t be able to touch. conceiving of what we fundamentally need that does not exist right now.

that’s a different faculty. the first one is prediction. the second one is imagination, in the specific sense frye meant.

one way to read the history of religion, and i want to be clear that this is a reading rather than a settled thing, is that these systems exist to hold up whatever a people needs held up at the time. whatever model of the world, whatever way of coping, whatever explanation makes the era survivable. and when a society’s understanding outgrows the model, the model gets replaced. we lean on a structure until we no longer fit inside it. that pattern is so evident in technology it’s almost funny. we need this tool, we build our whole workflow around it, and then something arrives that makes the need itself look different.

i think something like chatgpt was that. a genuinely big jump, and it wasn’t a jump because it was chasing the next product on the board. it landed because it answered something we actually wanted and had never quite been able to ask for, something that meets us at the level of our own thinking.

and i think a lot of what’s getting built right now is slop, not because the people building it are stupid, but because the race makes it almost impossible to ask a slower question. when your whole reward system is speed, you optimize for the thing next to you, not the thing nobody has conceptualized yet.

## okay wait

i’m not sure. i feel like maybe this is just me preaching something i already believe and building the argument backwards from it.

because here’s the obvious objection, and if you’ve read this far you’ve probably already had it: plenty of people who have never finished a novel have built enormous things. and plenty of extremely well read people have never built anything at all. i can’t pretend that isn’t true.

so i asked myself who i actually know who builds beautifully and doesn’t read. and the honest answer is that the ones who build well *and* have some balance in their lives, they outsource their philosophy from somewhere else. they have people around them who have those critical conversations with them. or they were brought up in a way where someone cultivated that habit early. it’s not the books. the books are one delivery mechanism.

so the claim can’t be *read novels.* the claim is that there’s a faculty for stepping outside your own frame, and literature is one of the ways in.

and then i know people who build really well and don’t have any of that. and my instinct was to say, yeah, but look at their lives. narrow focus, short horizon, burning out.

wait. that’s not actually an answer to the question, is it.

the question was whether you can build without this. i answered whether you can be happy without it. i changed the subject mid sentence and almost didn’t notice, so let me figure out what i’m really claiming here.

## what i’m actually claiming

i think i set out to make an argument about capability and i landed somewhere else.

the honest version is that i don’t know if the educated imagination makes you better at building. it might. the compass thing from that stage still rings true to me and i’d defend it. but i can’t prove it, and i’ve watched enough counterexamples to be suspicious of anyone who says they can.

what i can actually say is smaller and i believe it completely. chasing purely technical things, internships, tools, the next launch, at some point you’re done with all of that, and then what feeds you? i’ve written before about [how much of what we consume is engineered to take your attention](https://nabirarashid.substack.com/p/your-energy-is-the-most-expensive) and give nothing back. literature was the thing that actually fed me. it’s so thought provoking to apply these questions to everything, watching a film and asking why is this a convention in this genre, why is this a pattern, why does this keep showing up. that’s enriching in a way that i need to code this, i need to code that never was.

and it’s not a hobby you bolt onto a career. it’s more like a lifestyle. you can be extremely ambitious and also wise about how you carry it. you don’t just need to be technical to build something, you need to be reasonably whole. problem solving, managing your own head, knowing what you’re doing it for.

[my last piece](https://nabirarashid.substack.com/p/i-finally-see-the-glass) was about noticing the container you’re in, the walls you can’t see because you’ve never had a thought from outside them. this is the part i left out: noticing the container doesn’t tell you what’s past it. imagination is the thing that does. that’s what actually gets you out of the small bubble long enough to see what genuinely matters, in your life and in the world.

## so what do you see, then

which brings me to the question i’ve been avoiding for this entire essay.

if the whole argument is that this faculty lets you conceive of what doesn’t exist yet, then fine: what do i see? what’s the world i want to live in and what’s missing from it?

and the honest answer is i don’t know yet.

i’m eighteen. i’ve been feeding this thing for about a year. i can feel that it’s there, i can feel it changing what i notice, i just can’t lift anything with it yet. and i think that’s actually consistent with what i’m arguing, because frye’s whole point is that this is a trained faculty, not a talent you either have or don’t. it takes years of material going in before anything useful comes out.

so this isn’t the essay where i tell you what to build. it’s the one where i admit i spent a year thinking fiction was noise, and that it turned out to be the thing quietly teaching me how to see.

you can’t build what you can’t picture. i’m still learning how to picture.
