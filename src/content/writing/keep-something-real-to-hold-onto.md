a few months ago a close friend wanted to talk to me about ai art. she had a lot of feelings about it and wanted to know where i stood, and i told her honestly that i didn’t have a stand yet. i was still developing one. i think that’s a better thing to say than faking a position.

i can now tell you the exact moment i got one.

## before

when ai-generated art first started blowing up, i didn’t really connect with the issue deeply. it just wasn’t a soft spot for me, so as selfish as it sounds, i didn’t have a big problem with it. i figured human art would always be the thing that’s actually valued and ai art was a shortcut that would sort itself out.

i wasn’t defending it. i just wasn’t bothered.

## the song

i started my 1A term at waterloo and i’m already loving it, even though it’s only been about two weeks. and when i went home to visit my parents last weekend, we were driving somewhere and they played me a song.

*Photo by [Travis Yewell](https://unsplash.com/@shutters_guild) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com)*

some context on what music is to me. normally i listen to gracie abrams, olivia rodrigo, 80s stuff, and pakistani and urdu songs. the urdu music especially has such high nostalgic value for me. listening to those songs instantly gives me flashbacks to being a kid, sitting in the car on long family drives. and now that i’ve actually moved out for college, that music has gotten more important, not less. i can genuinely tear up listening to it. it carries an emotional weight my other genres don’t always hit.

so we’re in the car, which is the exact place that music lives for me, and my parents play a song made to mimic the style of a really popular artist in the urdu music industry. when they told me it was ai-generated i was like, sure, go ahead, let me listen.

and when i actually heard it i felt physically repulsed. i don’t know how else to explain it. i was genuinely disgusted.

sound-wise it was fine. it followed all the conventions and formulas of that style, and besides some weird phrasing here and there it sounded pretty good. but something felt so deeply off.

i think what disturbed me was realizing there wasn’t a real person behind it. no human soul, no genuine emotion, no actual story being shared. i was having a feeling about something with nothing behind it, and it made me feel empty.

and the weirdest part is i was the only one in the car who felt that way. my parents thought it was creative and impressive.

## so what was that

i’m taking a philosophy course this term, and about a week later we covered a concept that gave me language for it.

it comes from harry frankfurt, who wrote an essay called on bullshit, and the argument is that bullshit isn’t the same as lying. a liar is actually engaged with the truth. they know what’s true and they’re steering you away from it on purpose, which means they’re playing the same game as an honest person, just from the other side. a bullshitter isn’t playing that game at all. they’re not aiming at the truth and they’re not aiming away from it. they just don’t care what’s true. the indifference is the whole thing.

and here’s the part that stopped me. what makes something bullshit isn’t that it’s false, it’s that it’s phony. and a phony thing doesn’t have to be worse than the real thing in any way except that it isn’t real. it can be an exact copy.

that’s the song. technically fine, all the conventions, sounded good. the defect wasn’t in what it was, it was in how it was made.

which also means a language model is a purer case of this than any person frankfurt was writing about. a human bullshitter at least has the option of caring about truth and turns it down. a model doesn’t have a relationship to truth to turn down.

there’s a newer version of the idea too, that what makes something bullshit is less about how it treats the truth and more about how it treats you, that the person talking doesn’t care whether you believe them. which honestly complicates my whole reaction, because with a generated song there’s nobody who doesn’t care. there’s just nobody. that one i’m still chewing on.

## okay, two things

i was told it was ai before i heard it. so i don’t actually know whether i’d have felt any of that if nobody had said anything. i’d love to tell you i would have. i can’t.

and my parents weren’t bothered, and this is their music in a way it isn’t quite mine. their nostalgia is the original, mine is inherited through those car rides. two people with deeper roots in that tradition heard exactly what i heard and thought it was impressive. so there’s a real possibility i’m the outlier here rather than the one seeing clearly.

i’m not going to pretend either of those away. but i also don’t think being able to detect it is the point, and i want to explain why, because it’s the actual thing i’ve been trying to say.

## the part that scares me

the more of this we consume, the more exhausting it gets to work out what’s real. and people don’t stay in that state forever. they get tired and go passive, or they decide everything is fake and stop trying. either way someone benefits from a population that no longer knows what to believe, and it isn’t the population.

you can already see it in the low stakes stuff. those ai videos that go around where nobody is even pretending they’re real, the fruit ones and whatever else is trending this month. nobody is deceived. everyone knows. they spread anyway. so the problem was never really deception, which is why knowing doesn’t fix it.

and art matters here because of what art has been doing for us this whole time. music, painting, writing are raw expressions of our collective stories, our history, our lessons, the emotions everyone gets handed eventually. love and grief and jealousy. they’re how we stay in contact with what it actually means to be human, across generations of people we’ll never meet.

that contact is a baseline. it’s the thing you measure everything else against. algorithms and echo chambers were already pushing us around, but you can notice you’re being pushed as long as you have something fixed to check yourself against. lose the anchor and there’s nothing left to measure with. you can be moved in any direction and not feel it happening, and at that point whoever wants to move you can.

[my last piece](https://nabirarashid.substack.com/p/i-finally-see-the-glass) was about walls you can’t see, and how the dangerous ones are dangerous precisely because they don’t feel like walls. this is the same problem from the other side. without something real to hold yourself against, you never notice the room is moving.

## and me

i should say the obvious thing before someone says it for me. i’m a cs student, i work in ai, i use these tools most days and i intend to build with them.

so where’s my line? the best i’ve got is that it depends on what the thing is for. nobody listens to a function for the soul behind it. code is a tool and i don’t need a person on the other end of a tool. but art is somebody telling you something, and the whole transaction depends on there being a somebody. remove the person from a function and you get a faster function. remove the person from a song and you get an object shaped like a song.

i’m not fully sure that line holds. i can already feel where it gets blurry and i don’t want to pretend it’s cleaner than it is.

## so what do we actually do

not detection. i don’t think the answer is getting really good at spotting what’s generated, and i don’t think ai-generated work is going to be banned or that i’d want it to be.

the answer i keep landing on is much more boring and much harder. stay in direct contact with the real thing. keep listening to the songs somebody wrote because something happened to them. read the book a person sat with for years. sit with the art that was made by a human being trying to say something true, on purpose, to other human beings.

not out of purity, and not so you can win an argument about whether a track was generated. because that contact is the only thing keeping your sense of what’s human calibrated, and a person with a calibrated sense of what’s human is much harder to push around. that’s the whole defense. it’s not a policy, it’s a practice, and it’s the one thing in this entire situation that’s actually up to us.

advancing this technology is genuinely amazing and i’m not going to pretend otherwise, i’m going to spend my life on it. but it’s turning into something that can be used to move people, and the people who’d like to move you are not going to stop and ask whether you have your bearings.

because the scary part was never that there was nobody on the other end of that one song. it’s who gets to steer us once we’ve stopped being able to feel the difference.

so keep something real to hold onto. that’s the whole thing.
