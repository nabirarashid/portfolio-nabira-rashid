there’s an idea from my philosophy course that has been living in my head rent free for weeks, and the longer it sits there, the more it looks like an explanation for most of what feels broken about how we talk to each other. so let me think out loud about it.

## the rules of the game

the idea is the norms of assertion, and it sounds dry until you see what it’s actually describing.

when you assert something, you’re also signing up for obligations. you’re saying: i believe this, i’m not trying to trick you, i have evidence for it, and i’m making this claim against the most current understanding we have, what philosophers call the state of the art. and the big one: if you bring me relevant evidence against my claim, i have to either answer it or take it back. defend or retract.

that accountability is the whole machine. an argument, in the philosophy sense, is the basic unit of rational exchange, two minds trading reasons instead of volume, and the norms of assertion are what keep that unit working. a community that plays by these rules is an epistemic community, and the magic is that everyone in it can disagree, loudly, constantly, and still be building one shared picture of the world. the disagreements are a feature. same evidence, different weights, argue it out, that’s how the picture improves. but there’s a requirement hiding underneath: the arguing only works if both sides are standing on the same base of knowledge.

and i’ll be honest, when we covered this in class i had alarm bells ringing in my head. like, damn, this is dangerous. because the moment you understand what holds an epistemic community together, you start seeing how many things in modern life are quietly taking it apart, and almost none of them do it by making us disagree more. they go after the foundation, the shared picture itself.

i started counting the ways. i got to three before i ran out of lecture.

## station one: the inputs

here’s the oldest observation i have about the internet, mine in the sense that i noticed it before anyone explained it: search the same thing as someone in a different country, or honestly just a different person in the same city, and you get different results. different sources, different framings, different dates of being updated. we were taught this as a fun fact when we were kids, and i feel like it might be the least fun fact i know.

because the obvious question is: why? why is it so hard to just show everyone the same content, ranked? and the uncomfortable answer is that “just ranked” doesn’t exist. ranking requires a ranker, somebody deciding what counts as relevant, what’s trustworthy, what gets seen first. strip away all the personalization and you still don’t get a neutral view of the world. you get the platform’s judgment calls, somebody’s picture of what matters, just not one tuned to me. and that somebody is optimizing for attention, not for the thing we’re all supposed to be building together.

and the search bar was the tame version. the feeds we actually live in now run the same mechanism continuously, on everything. so before anyone has asserted anything, the ground is already tilted. two people can follow the rules perfectly, believe honestly, cite their evidence, update when challenged, and still talk straight past each other, because their evidence arrived through inputs that don’t overlap. we’re not really one epistemic community having an argument anymore. we’re subscribers to different stations.

i’ve [written before](https://nabirarashid.substack.com/p/keep-something-real-to-hold-onto) about what it costs when we lose the shared baseline we measure everything against. this is that argument’s missing first chapter. the baseline doesn’t erode because people get dumber. it erodes because the inputs stop overlapping.

## station two: the exemption

the second case is different in kind, and i want to be careful with it, because i’m not making the internet-atheist argument.

faith, as it’s usually defined, is believing without needing evidence. and that’s on purpose. it’s what makes faith faith instead of just a conclusion. but run a faith claim through the assertion rules and something odd happens. it can’t be asked for its evidence, because not needing any is what it’s made of. it can’t be answered with contrary evidence, because it was never resting on evidence. defend-or-retract never attaches. faith functions as an exemption from the norms of assertion.

i’m nowhere near the first person stuck here. w.k. clifford argued in the 1870s that believing on insufficient evidence is always wrong, william james fired back that life’s biggest questions won’t wait for receipts, and the fight still isn’t settled a hundred and fifty years later, which i find weirdly comforting.

and to be precise: faith doesn’t break the rules of assertion. it sits outside them by design, and it’s been there far longer than the rules have had a name. so the complication is really a coordination one: a community trying to build one picture now has to do it across a line where the tools work on one side and simply don’t apply on the other. none of that is a verdict on anyone. i feel like pretending this is simple, in either direction, is how the conversation always goes bad.

## station three: the rulebook fight

the third case is the deepest one, and it’s where i think most of our political exhaustion actually lives.

the assertion rules say you make claims against the state of the art. but that assumes everyone agrees on what counts as current. and increasingly, we don’t.

take the example we’re all living inside of: the liberal-conservative divide. it gets described as a fight about issues, but underneath almost every issue is the same meta-fight. which sources count. which authorities get to update the state of the art. which era’s understanding is the baseline we reason from. the conservative instinct anchors to an older and time-tested set of norms. the progressive instinct keeps revising toward the newest one. that’s just what the words mean, and i’m genuinely not scoring either here, anchoring can preserve wisdom or preserve harm, revising can be progress or churn. the point is that each side experiences itself as epistemically responsible, because within its own frame it is. so when the two sides fight about any particular issue, they’re really fighting about whose version of current counts.

that’s not a disagreement the defend-or-retract game can referee, because the sides can’t even agree on what would qualify as relevant evidence. it’s a fight about the rulebook happening in the middle of the game, and the rules of assertion have no rule for deciding whose rules count.

this, i think, is why so much public argument feels like screaming into weather. everyone involved thinks they’re disagreeing about claims, when the real disagreement is about norms, one level down, where nobody’s looking. and when the knowledge bases don’t overlap, there’s no room to argue at all. the unit of rational exchange stops exchanging.

## the part where it turns around on me

so that’s the tidy version: three stations, three ways the shared picture fragments, me narrating from somewhere above it all.

except i’ve also been reading plato on my own lately, and the cave allegory from republic book seven keeps ruining my aerial view. prisoners chained up watching shadows on a wall, one escapes, sees the real world, comes back, nobody believes him. the famous point is that the prisoners don’t know it’s a cave. the shadows aren’t a theory they hold, they just are reality to them.

but here’s what i keep snagging on. plato wrote the story with a guaranteed outside, his freed prisoner really had seen the sun. we don’t get that guarantee, just a lot of people certain they’re the one who got out. and the prisoner who has actually seen the sun and the prisoner who’s merely sure he has feel exactly the same from the inside. so my confidence that i’m outside is not evidence that i’m outside. it’s the only feeling in the whole story that everyone shares.

because here’s the thing: this entire essay is a broadcast from a station. my station. secular, evidence-first, philosophy course, substack feed full of people who already think like me. there’s a distinction from the philosopher c. thi nguyen that haunts me here. an epistemic bubble just lacks exposure and pops on contact with new information. an echo chamber actively metabolizes contrary evidence, every attack on the belief becomes more proof of it. when i learned that, i immediately, comfortably, filed other communities under echo chamber. it took me embarrassingly long to turn the question around. if something showed up that my frame wasn’t built to see, would it let the evidence in, or quietly convert it into one more example of how right i already am? i don’t actually know. the test i would use to check whether my test is any good is the test.

so i can’t end this by telling you whose station has the real signal. claiming that would just be my station doing what stations do.

## what’s left, then

something smaller, but i think it’s real.

most of the fights that exhaust us aren’t happening where we think they are. before getting pulled into one, it’s worth asking which problem you’re actually looking at. different evidence? that’s fixable, share it. same evidence, different weights? that’s a real disagreement, the healthy kind, have it. no shared rules for what counts in the first place? then no amount of arguing the claim will touch it, and knowing that won’t win you the fight, but it saves you from mistaking weather for conversation.

and the personal version, the only part i fully control: keep checking what my station isn’t playing. read the things my feed would never serve me. that doesn’t get me out of the cave, and i can’t even verify it’s helping. but there’s a difference between a prisoner who’s certain he’s seen the sun and one who’s at least found the chains interesting.

because the scariest fights of this decade aren’t the ones where we disagree. they’re the ones where we’re not even disagreeing.
